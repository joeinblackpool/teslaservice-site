Lease vs buy: the trade-offs

Neither is automatically right — here's what genuinely differs and who each suits.

Last fact-checked: 28 September 2026. General guidance — always check your own model's specifics and current official sources before relying on it for a decision.

The core trade-off

What's the fundamental difference?

Buying (outright or on finance you'll own at the end) means you carry the depreciation risk and benefit if the car holds value better than expected, but also lose out if it depreciates faster — as covered in our owner tips page, Tesla depreciation has become less predictable than its earlier reputation suggested. Leasing fixes your cost for a known period and hands the depreciation risk to the leasing company, in exchange for you never owning the car and typically facing mileage limits and end-of-contract condition charges.

Who leasing suits

When does leasing make more sense?

Leasing tends to suit drivers who want a predictable monthly cost, don't want to deal with resale, and are comfortable switching to a newer model every few years — which also means always having current software/hardware and a fresh warranty. It's less suitable if you drive high annual mileage (excess mileage charges add up) or want to heavily customise or keep a car long-term.

Who buying suits

When does buying make more sense?

Buying suits drivers planning to keep the car for many years past any typical lease term, who drive unusually high mileage where lease penalties would be costly, or who value building equity in an asset rather than a pure running cost. It carries more risk if the car depreciates faster than expected, but also more upside if it doesn't.
